Beach by beach in Ireland

Keem Bay, Achill Island (4.6 / 5)

Keem Bay is a horseshoe of pale sand and clear blue-green water at the western end of Achill Island, enclosed by steep green hills. The road down is narrow and winding, with sweeping views over the Atlantic.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Moderate.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, no shops or cafes at the beach. Getting there and parking: Car park above the beach; it fills on sunny days.

Dog's Bay, Roundstone (4.5 / 5)

Dog's Bay is a crescent of brilliant white sand made largely of tiny shells, on one side of a narrow sandy neck. Gurteen Bay lies on the other side, so you can choose the more sheltered beach on a windy day.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: No facilities on the beach; shops and pubs in Roundstone. Getting there and parking: Small car park and roadside parking near the beach.

Derrynane Beach (4.5 / 5)

Derrynane has white sand and clear water in a sheltered bay on the Ring of Kerry, next to Derrynane House, the home of Daniel O'Connell, now a national historic park. At low tide you can walk to Abbey Island and its ruined abbey.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, toilets, cafe nearby. Getting there and parking: Car park in the national historic park.

Inch Beach (4.4 / 5)

Inch Beach is a sand spit about 5 km long reaching across Dingle Bay, with dunes behind and mountains on every side. Gentle surf makes it a good place to learn, and it featured in films such as Ryan's Daughter.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, cafe, surf school and board hire. Getting there and parking: Parking near the beach entrance.

Coumeenoole Beach, Slea Head (4.4 / 5)

Coumeenoole is a small sandy cove at the western tip of the Dingle Peninsula, reached by a steep path from the Slea Head Drive, with views to the Blasket Islands. The setting is spectacular, but the currents here are strong.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 14 degrees C. Access: Moderate.

Facilities: No facilities. Getting there and parking: Small car park above the cove on the Slea Head Drive.

Barleycove Beach (4.4 / 5)

Barleycove is a wide sandy bay backed by high dunes near Mizen Head, the most south-westerly point of Ireland. A river estuary crosses the beach, and the dunes are a protected habitat.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, toilets near the car park. Getting there and parking: Car park near the beach.

Curracloe Beach (4.3 / 5)

Curracloe is a long sandy beach on the Wexford coast, backed by dunes and Raven Wood. Its opening scenes stood in for Omaha Beach in the film Saving Private Ryan. The south-east is one of the sunniest corners of Ireland.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 16 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, toilets at the main entrances. Getting there and parking: Several free car parks along the beach.

Lahinch Beach (4.2 / 5)

Lahinch is a lively surf town with a long sandy beach on Liscannor Bay, and the Cliffs of Moher are a short drive away. Several surf schools work from the promenade, and the waves suit both beginners and experienced surfers.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, surf schools, cafes and toilets on the promenade. Getting there and parking: Paid parking on the promenade.

Rossnowlagh Beach (4.2 / 5)

Rossnowlagh is a long, wide beach of firm sand on Donegal Bay, popular with surfers and families. Gentle, consistent waves make it one of the best places in Ireland to learn to surf.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 14 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, surf school, hotel and cafe by the beach. Getting there and parking: Parking near the beach.

Velvet Strand, Portmarnock (4.1 / 5)

The Velvet Strand is a long beach of firm, smooth sand north of Dublin, with views to Ireland's Eye and Lambay Island. In 1930 Charles Kingsford Smith took off from here on a transatlantic flight.

Best season: June–August. Typical water temperature: about 15 degrees C. Access: Easy.

Facilities: Lifeguards at set times in summer, toilets and cafes nearby. Getting there and parking: Parking along the coast road; DART trains to Portmarnock.

About beaches in Ireland

Ireland has thousands of kilometres of Atlantic and Irish Sea coast, with wide sandy strands, white shell-sand coves and surf beaches. The west, from Donegal through Connemara to Kerry and Cork, has the most dramatic scenery, while the south-east is drier and sunnier.

When to go

The beach season is short, from June to August, with the longest days around June. The sea is cold, usually around 14 to 16 C in summer, so many swimmers wear wetsuits. Weather changes fast, so pack layers even in July.

Getting around

A car is the easiest way to reach the west coast beaches, as many are at the end of narrow country roads. Dublin, Shannon, Cork and Knock have airports, and DART trains serve the beaches north and south of Dublin.

Good to know

The currency is the euro. Lifeguards cover many popular beaches at set times in summer, so check the times at the beach. Surf schools work on most Atlantic beaches, and the Wild Atlantic Way route links many of the best ones.

Frequently asked questions

Can you swim in the sea in Ireland?

Yes, but the water is cold, usually 14 to 16 C in summer. Swim at lifeguarded beaches, and consider a wetsuit.

Which Irish beaches are best for learning to surf?

Lahinch, Rossnowlagh and Inch have gentle, consistent waves and several surf schools.
